Information science is the study of how information is organized, stored, retrieved, and communicated, also the people, systems, and institutions that carry out those tasks. It draws on library science, computer science, cognitive science, linguistics, and management science, but is unified by a narrower concern than any of those fields alone: how a given piece of information moves from creation to a person who needs it.

The discipline is often described through three core activities—representing knowledge in a structured form, organizing that structure so it can be searched, and retrieving relevant material from it on demand. Around this core sit more specialized concerns, from the quantitative study of information production and use, to the policies and institutions that govern how information circulates in society, to the practices organizations use to capture and share knowledge that has not yet been written down.

Because it treats information as its object of study rather than any one technology for producing it, information science has repeatedly absorbed new territory as the means of recording and transmitting knowledge have changed—from card catalogs to databases to the web. Its divisions below reflect that layered history: some, like library science, are the field's oldest institutional home; others, like ontology, reflect concerns that only became central once machines began doing the organizing.
